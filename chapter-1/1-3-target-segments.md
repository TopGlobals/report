## 1.3. Target Segments

<table>
	<thead>
		<tr>
			<th>Attribute</th>
			<th>Segment 1: Bioclinical and Laboratory Personnel</th>
			<th>Segment 2: Technical and Maintenance Personnel</th>
		</tr>
	</thead>
	<tbody>
		<tr>
			<td><strong>Context & Statistical Support</strong></td>
			<td>They represent the primary users of the application, responsible for processing biological samples. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), up to 25% of clinical vaccines and reagents are degraded globally due to cold chain logistics failures. Their main administrative burden lies in manual documentation tasks, taking valuable time away from clinical analysis. They require an application that provides immediate location tracking (RFID) and thermal status to prevent these clinical risks.</td>
			<td>They are the specialists in charge of ensuring the operability of the laboratory's infrastructure. Statistically, in medium-sized healthcare facilities, over 40% of technical support dispatches are categorized as "false alarms" (e.g., doors left open by staff). Their technical criteria are heavily relied upon by management to validate software purchases. They need remote monitoring software to reduce these inefficiencies and optimize maintenance routes.</td>
		</tr>
		<tr>
			<td><strong>Demographics</strong></td>
			<td>Men and women between 20 and 50 years old. They possess higher education degrees (Biology, Chemistry, Medical Technology, or Biotechnology). They range from junior laboratory analysts to senior clinical coordinators. They act as the critical end-users whose adoption dictates the success of the software.</td>
			<td>Men and women between 20 and 50 years old. They hold higher technical education or engineering degrees (Biomedical Engineering, Electronics, or Systems). In the institutional hierarchy, they act as key Decision Influencers who evaluate the technical feasibility and reliability of the platform.</td>
		</tr>
		<tr>
			<td><strong>Psychographics (Pains & Gains)</strong></td>
			<td>They work under constant pressure to avoid diagnostic errors and fear surprise regulatory audits. They are meticulous, highly analytical individuals whose greatest professional fear is the validation of a false negative derived from a degraded reagent. Their primary goal (Gain) is to achieve clinical peace of mind and eliminate the stress of manual tracking.</td>
			<td>They work under a reactive demand model and high stress levels. Their daily routine is unpredictable, often interrupting scheduled tasks to handle emergencies. Their main frustration (Pain) is traveling to a site without context of the failure. Their goal (Gain) is to achieve operational efficiency, looking for tools that allow them to anticipate incidents.</td>
		</tr>
		<tr>
			<td><strong>Behavioral & Technographics</strong></td>
			<td>They waste significant time physically searching for misplaced inventory. Technographically, they are heavy smartphone users but struggle with outdated hospital PCs. They need clean, fast web interfaces to quickly search the RFID location of a sample, confirm its thermal status, and export immutable logbooks. They will reject any software that presents a high learning curve or complex hardware configurations.</td>
			<td>They are digitally competent and pragmatically technological. In their personal lives, they consume high-interactivity mobile apps. They interact with the software layer primarily to receive precise, contextualized Push notifications about which specific refrigerator or sensor is failing. They use the platform's dashboard as a triage tool to decide what replacement parts to bring before traveling.</td>
		</tr>
	</tbody>
</table>
